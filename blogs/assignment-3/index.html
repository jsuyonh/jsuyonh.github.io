When Hiring Technology Discriminates: Fairness, Accountability, and the Human Cost of Automated Recruitment

October 31, 2026 · Assignment 3 · AI Ethics · AI Hiring

Introduction

Technology is changing the way companies recruit employees. Many organizations now use automated systems and artificial intelligence (AI) to review resumes, filter applications, and support hiring decisions. These tools can save time and reduce administrative work.

However, an important question remains: Can technology make recruitment more efficient without making it less fair?

Automated recruitment does not automatically guarantee equal opportunities. If a system uses discriminatory rules or biased information, it can produce unfair results. Luciano Floridi (2023) explains that ethical AI requires more than technical performance. It also requires fairness, transparency, accountability, and responsible governance.

This blog explores the ethical challenges of automated recruitment, examines the iTutorGroup case, and presents practical recommendations for employers.

1. How Automated Recruitment Works

Automated recruitment uses digital systems to support different stages of hiring. These systems can:

  • Scan resumes and identify relevant skills.
  • Filter applications according to specific requirements.
  • Rank candidates and schedule interviews.
  • Analyze assessment results.

Some systems use AI and machine learning, while others follow predefined rules programmed by people. Not every automated system is necessarily an AI system.

These technologies can make recruitment faster, but their results depend on how they are designed and used. For example, if a company has historically hired more men than women, a system trained on previous hiring data could reproduce this pattern and disadvantage female applicants.

This demonstrates that technology can reproduce existing inequalities instead of eliminating them.

2. Real-World Case Study: iTutorGroup

A real example of discriminatory automated recruitment involved iTutorGroup, an online tutoring company.

According to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the company used application software programmed to automatically reject female applicants aged 55 or older and male applicants aged 60 or older.

The EEOC reported that more than 200 qualified applicants in the United States were rejected because of these age-related rules. In May 2022, the EEOC filed a lawsuit against iTutorGroup. In September 2023, the company agreed to pay US$365,000 to settle the age discrimination lawsuit.

This case demonstrates that automated decisions are not automatically neutral or fair. It is also important to clarify that the EEOC described software programmed with discriminatory rules; the available information does not establish that these rules were learned through machine learning.

The main lesson is that employers remain responsible for the decisions and rules implemented through their technology.

3. Algorithmic Fairness: What Does Fair Hiring Mean?

Algorithmic fairness concerns whether automated systems treat individuals and groups fairly. Floridi (2023) discusses different approaches to understanding fairness:

1. Anti-classification: Protected characteristics, such as age or sex, should not be explicitly used in decisions. However, proxy variables may indirectly reveal similar characteristics. For example, a postal code could be associated with a particular demographic group.

2. Classification parity: This approach examines whether prediction errors, such as false positives and false negatives, are comparable across different groups.

3. Calibration: Predictions should have comparable reliability for different groups. The same prediction should have a similar meaning for each group.

4. Statistical parity: This approach examines whether different groups receive similar rates of positive outcomes, such as being selected for an interview.

These approaches show that fairness can be measured in different ways. However, they have limitations and may sometimes conflict. There is no single measure that guarantees fairness in every situation. Therefore, companies should regularly examine how their recruitment systems affect different groups.

4. Applying Five Ethical Principles to Recruitment

Floridi (2023) presents five principles for evaluating AI: beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability.

  • Beneficence: Technology should provide benefits. In recruitment, it should help identify qualified candidates and improve the hiring process, not simply reduce costs.
  • Non-maleficence: Organizations should prevent harm. Candidates should not be rejected because of age, sex, or characteristics unrelated to their ability to perform the job.
  • Autonomy: Candidates should have meaningful opportunities to participate in the hiring process. Important decisions should not depend entirely on automated systems.
  • Justice: Employers should provide equal opportunities and ensure that recruitment criteria are relevant to the position.
  • Explicability: Decisions should be understandable. Candidates and employers should be able to understand how a system works and identify who is responsible for its decisions.

Together, these principles provide an ethical foundation for responsible recruitment.

5. Soft Ethics, Accountability, and Human Oversight

Soft ethics uses ethical principles and responsible practices to guide technology beyond basic legal requirements.

Floridi (2023) explains that ethics, regulation, and governance have different but complementary roles:

  • Ethics evaluates what is morally acceptable.
  • Regulation establishes legal requirements.
  • Governance organizes the policies and responsibilities needed to apply these principles.

Following the law is necessary, but it does not always guarantee ethical behavior. One useful approach is an Ethical Impact Analysis (EIA), which examines the possible consequences of a digital system before and during its implementation. In recruitment, it can identify risks involving discrimination, privacy, transparency, and accountability.

Human oversight is also essential. Companies should not blame technology when an automated system produces an unfair result. People and organizations remain responsible for selecting, monitoring, and using these systems.

6. Practical Recommendations for Employers

Organizations can take several steps to promote fair recruitment:

  1. Review recruitment criteria: Ensure that selection criteria are relevant to the job and do not unfairly exclude candidates.
  2. Conduct fairness audits: Regularly examine results across different groups to identify possible discrimination.
  3. Protect privacy: Explain what personal information is collected and how it is used.
  4. Maintain human oversight: Allow qualified professionals to review and correct automated decisions.
  5. Improve transparency: Inform candidates when automated tools are used and provide ways to ask questions or request a review.
  6. Perform an Ethical Impact Analysis: Identify possible risks before introducing a recruitment system and continue evaluating its effects.

These actions can help companies make recruitment more responsible and respectful of candidates' rights.

Conclusion

Automated recruitment offers important opportunities, but it also creates ethical challenges. The iTutorGroup case demonstrates that software can exclude qualified candidates through discriminatory rules, even when the process appears objective and efficient.

Floridi's five ethical principles provide a useful framework for evaluating recruitment technology. Soft ethics, governance, accountability, transparency, and human oversight can also help organizations prevent harm.

A recruitment system should not be judged only by how quickly it selects candidates, but also by how fairly it treats people.

Technology should support better hiring decisions, but fairness requires human responsibility, continuous evaluation, and a genuine commitment to equal opportunities.
