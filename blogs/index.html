Data Science Ethics

Introduction: Can a survey really be anonymous?

As a data analyst, I am hired by an educational institution to process the results of a survey that students are required to complete at the end of a course. Students receive a 10-question survey; to complete it, they must access a specific URL in their web browser—which is available only for a short period—and log in using their organizational username and password.

At the beginning of the survey, the course name is requested, but the participant’s name is not. Meanwhile, the page description states that all responses and the survey itself are anonymous.

The executive leadership team intends to use the survey results to determine which courses need to be redesigned or updated.

As a data analyst, my role is to provide the executive team with actionable insights that will enable them to take further steps.

The organization has a legitimate objective. However, the fact that a business purpose is useful does not automatically mean that every method used for data collection or analysis is ethical. Therefore, the ethical issue is not merely whether the survey is anonymous, but whether that claim is accurate, understandable, and supported by the underlying system.

In this post, I will provide commentary from the perspective of a data analyst, drawing on the text by Martens, D. (2022), “Data Science Ethics: Concepts, Techniques, and Cautionary Tales”. Their FAT Flow framework considers the various stages of a data project and asks whether the practices are fair, accountable, and transparent.

1. Ethical issues

  • Privacy: Privacy is a component of fairness. Ethical data science requires balancing data utility with ethical concerns, and privacy is also a human right. The requirement to log in creates a potential link between a student’s identity and their participation.
  • Transparency: Participants should be informed about what information is being collected, why a login is required, whether identifying data is stored alongside the responses, who can access the original responses, and how the results will be presented.
  • Informed consent: The organization should explain the purpose of the survey and how the results will be used before students decide whether to participate.
  • Question design: The example question also warrants attention. A student might believe that most objectives were met, but that one significant objective was not. The question does not specify which objectives are being evaluated, and different students might interpret the scale in different ways. An ethical approach to assessment requires selecting questions and indicators that validly represent the aspect intended for evaluation.

2. Other ethical issues present in the survey

  • Re-identification: It may be possible to re-identify participants even after their names have been removed. Eliminating direct identifiers is often insufficient; a combination of attributes or knowledge of the context can reveal an individual’s identity.
  • Bias: This can affect who responds and how those responses are interpreted. Students who are highly satisfied or highly dissatisfied may be more likely to respond than those with moderate opinions. Students facing language barriers, accessibility needs, limited internet access, or a fear of retaliation may be less likely to participate. Consequently, the responses may not be representative of the entire class.
  • Data minimization: An organization should collect only the information necessary for its stated purpose. If the goal is to improve courses, the administration may need feedback on each course, but it likely does not require individual identities, detailed login histories, or unnecessary demographic information.
  • Purpose limitation and the progressive expansion of data uses: Students are informed that the survey will help identify courses in need of updates. The organization should not quietly repurpose these responses for unrelated objectives—such as penalizing individual instructors, making decisions about specific students, or profiling students’ attitudes.
  • Data security and access controls: The institution should determine who has access to the raw data, whether such access is logged, and when records are deleted. A promise alone does not protect privacy; technical and organizational measures are required.
  • Misleading interpretations and unintended consequences: If management publishes rankings without context, the results could damage reputations or incentivize instructors to prioritize popularity over learning. Decisions based on weak evidence could lead to unnecessary redesigns or fail to identify courses that are truly in need of improvement.

3. Recommendations for executive management

  • Verify the claim of anonymity before collecting responses. If identity data and responses are stored separately, this should be confirmed and documented. If the system allows them to be linked, the survey should not be described as anonymous; it should either be redesigned to prevent such linking or correctly labeled as “confidential,” with clear access restrictions.
  • Explain the process in plain language. Before the first question, students should be provided with a brief privacy notice explaining the survey’s purpose, what data is being collected, whether login information is kept separate from responses, who has access to the raw data, how the results will be used, how long the data will be retained, and whom to contact with questions. It should also state whether participation is voluntary and whether there are any consequences for declining to respond.
  • Implement data minimization and privacy-preserving preprocessing. Remove direct identifiers from the dataset whenever possible, limit or avoid the collection of unnecessary metadata, and suppress or aggregate results for very small groups. Free-text comments should be reviewed to remove identifying details before they are shared.
  • Improve the survey. Replace or supplement general questions with clear, specific questions linked to defined learning objectives. A space for comments can provide context, but students should be advised not to include identifying or sensitive information.
  • Establish governance and accountability. Assign clear responsibilities for survey administration, privacy protection, data access, analysis, and the approval of reports. Limit access to raw data to authorized personnel, use secure storage and transmission methods, log access, and establish a schedule for data retention and deletion.
  • Present results responsibly. Display aggregated results, explain limitations, and avoid making unsubstantiated claims about individual students or instructors.

4. What options are available to me to act ethically if my recommendations are rejected?

I can still act responsibly within my role by taking the following steps:

  • I would document the specific concern, the available evidence, the uncertainties, and the potential consequences. I would distinguish confirmed facts from mere possibilities. For example, I would not claim that the survey definitively identifies students unless the system’s design proves it does. Instead, I would explain that the login requirement raises an issue that must be verified before an absolute guarantee of anonymity can be justified.
  • I would present management with a concise assessment of risks and practical alternatives—such as decoupling authentication from response storage, restricting access to raw data, aggregating results, or revising the privacy notice.
  • I would seek guidance through the appropriate internal channels, such as the privacy officer, the information security team, an ethics committee, the instructor in charge of the course, or another designated governance body.
  • I would refuse to include unsubstantiated claims in my analysis or report. I would provide an honest account of the limitations and recommend that the findings be treated with caution until the issue is resolved.
  • Finally, I would consider formally escalating the matter in accordance with institutional policies and applicable professional obligations. I would not access student records without authorization, publicly disclose confidential information, or alter results to force a desired conclusion. Acting ethically means raising concerns through appropriate channels, protecting the individuals affected, and maintaining the integrity of the analysis. If asked to participate in deception or the misuse of data, I would seek proper guidance and refuse to carry out any specific action that conflicts with my professional responsibilities.

Scientists and data analysts make decisions regarding data, methods, interpretation, and communication. Even if a manager approves a project, the analyst remains responsible for honestly explaining what the evidence can—and cannot—demonstrate.

Conclusion: Reliable data requires reliable practices.

“Anonymous” is not just a label to be affixed to a form; it is a property that must be designed, verified, and maintained with integrity. Examining the project through the FAT Flow framework revealed issues regarding fairness in privacy, a lack of transparency, and unclear accountability—from data collection through to reporting. The best data scientists do not merely ask, “Can we do this?” but rather, “Should we, and how?” Answering this second question is what transforms a number on a scale from 0 to 10 into a decision that people can trust.

Ethical data science is not simply about gathering enough information to decide; it is about making decisions in a way that respects the individuals represented by that data.

AI Hiring

Introduction

Technology is changing the way companies recruit employees. Many organizations now use automated systems and artificial intelligence (AI) to review resumes, filter applications, and support hiring decisions. These tools can save time and reduce administrative work.

However, an important question remains: Can technology make recruitment more efficient without making it less fair?

Automated recruitment does not automatically guarantee equal opportunities. If a system uses discriminatory rules or biased information, it can produce unfair results. Luciano Floridi (2023) explains that ethical AI requires more than technical performance. It also requires fairness, transparency, accountability, and responsible governance.

This blog explores the ethical challenges of automated recruitment, examines the iTutorGroup case, and presents practical recommendations for employers.

1. How Automated Recruitment Works

Automated recruitment uses digital systems to support different stages of hiring. These systems can:

  • Scan resumes and identify relevant skills.
  • Filter applications according to specific requirements.
  • Rank candidates and schedule interviews.
  • Analyze assessment results.

Some systems use AI and machine learning, while others follow predefined rules programmed by people. Not every automated system is necessarily an AI system.

These technologies can make recruitment faster, but their results depend on how they are designed and used. For example, if a company has historically hired more men than women, a system trained on previous hiring data could reproduce this pattern and disadvantage female applicants.

This demonstrates that technology can reproduce existing inequalities instead of eliminating them.

2. Real-World Case Study: iTutorGroup

A real example of discriminatory automated recruitment involved iTutorGroup, an online tutoring company.

According to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the company used application software programmed to automatically reject female applicants aged 55 or older and male applicants aged 60 or older.

The EEOC reported that more than 200 qualified applicants in the United States were rejected because of these age-related rules. In May 2022, the EEOC filed a lawsuit against iTutorGroup. In September 2023, the company agreed to pay US$365,000 to settle the age discrimination lawsuit.

This case demonstrates that automated decisions are not automatically neutral or fair. It is also important to clarify that the EEOC described software programmed with discriminatory rules; the available information does not establish that these rules were learned through machine learning.

The main lesson is that employers remain responsible for the decisions and rules implemented through their technology.

3. Algorithmic Fairness: What Does Fair Hiring Mean?

Algorithmic fairness concerns whether automated systems treat individuals and groups fairly. Floridi (2023) discusses different approaches to understanding fairness:

1. Anti-classification: Protected characteristics, such as age or sex, should not be explicitly used in decisions. However, proxy variables may indirectly reveal similar characteristics. For example, a postal code could be associated with a particular demographic group.

2. Classification parity: This approach examines whether prediction errors, such as false positives and false negatives, are comparable across different groups.

3. Calibration: Predictions should have comparable reliability for different groups. The same prediction should have a similar meaning for each group.

4. Statistical parity: This approach examines whether different groups receive similar rates of positive outcomes, such as being selected for an interview.

These approaches show that fairness can be measured in different ways. However, they have limitations and may sometimes conflict. There is no single measure that guarantees fairness in every situation. Therefore, companies should regularly examine how their recruitment systems affect different groups.

4. Applying Five Ethical Principles to Recruitment

Floridi (2023) presents five principles for evaluating AI: beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability.

  • Beneficence: Technology should provide benefits. In recruitment, it should help identify qualified candidates and improve the hiring process, not simply reduce costs.
  • Non-maleficence: Organizations should prevent harm. Candidates should not be rejected because of age, sex, or characteristics unrelated to their ability to perform the job.
  • Autonomy: Candidates should have meaningful opportunities to participate in the hiring process. Important decisions should not depend entirely on automated systems.
  • Justice: Employers should provide equal opportunities and ensure that recruitment criteria are relevant to the position.
  • Explicability: Decisions should be understandable. Candidates and employers should be able to understand how a system works and identify who is responsible for its decisions.

Together, these principles provide an ethical foundation for responsible recruitment.

5. Soft Ethics, Accountability, and Human Oversight

Soft ethics uses ethical principles and responsible practices to guide technology beyond basic legal requirements.

Floridi (2023) explains that ethics, regulation, and governance have different but complementary roles:

  • Ethics evaluates what is morally acceptable.
  • Regulation establishes legal requirements.
  • Governance organizes the policies and responsibilities needed to apply these principles.

Following the law is necessary, but it does not always guarantee ethical behavior. One useful approach is an Ethical Impact Analysis (EIA), which examines the possible consequences of a digital system before and during its implementation. In recruitment, it can identify risks involving discrimination, privacy, transparency, and accountability.

Human oversight is also essential. Companies should not blame technology when an automated system produces an unfair result. People and organizations remain responsible for selecting, monitoring, and using these systems.

6. Practical Recommendations for Employers

Organizations can take several steps to promote fair recruitment:

  1. Review recruitment criteria: Ensure that selection criteria are relevant to the job and do not unfairly exclude candidates.
  2. Conduct fairness audits: Regularly examine results across different groups to identify possible discrimination.
  3. Protect privacy: Explain what personal information is collected and how it is used.
  4. Maintain human oversight: Allow qualified professionals to review and correct automated decisions.
  5. Improve transparency: Inform candidates when automated tools are used and provide ways to ask questions or request a review.
  6. Perform an Ethical Impact Analysis: Identify possible risks before introducing a recruitment system and continue evaluating its effects.

These actions can help companies make recruitment more responsible and respectful of candidates' rights.

Conclusion

Automated recruitment offers important opportunities, but it also creates ethical challenges. The iTutorGroup case demonstrates that software can exclude qualified candidates through discriminatory rules, even when the process appears objective and efficient.

Floridi's five ethical principles provide a useful framework for evaluating recruitment technology. Soft ethics, governance, accountability, transparency, and human oversight can also help organizations prevent harm.

A recruitment system should not be judged only by how quickly it selects candidates, but also by how fairly it treats people.

Technology should support better hiring decisions, but fairness requires human responsibility, continuous evaluation, and a genuine commitment to equal opportunities.
